# Project-wide Design Guardrails

## Purpose / authority

This document preserves the original Central Brain + project-owner rules about what this RPG should **not** become, plus a small number of later principles derived from additional game-design review.

These are project-wide guardrails, not narrow system rules. They apply across combat, exploration, quests, narrative, progression, crafting, UI, economy, world design, and production decisions.

If a future proposal conflicts with one of these rules, do not silently ignore the guardrail. Flag the conflict for Central Brain and decide deliberately whether the rule still stands.

## Core guardrails

1. **No content treadmill that makes old progress worthless.** We don't want the classic MMO problem where a new update makes yesterday's weapons, areas, materials, and accomplishments garbage. New progression should add possibilities, sidegrades, knowledge, uses, interactions, or genuine upgrades without constantly deleting the value of the past.

2. **No giant open world that is technically free but functionally directionless.** Freedom is good. “Here are 80 destinations, good luck figuring out why you care” is not. The player should always have understandable threads they can follow while still being free to ignore them.

3. **No instant-teleport-everywhere design that destroys geography.** Convenience should not turn the world into disconnected rooms joined by menus. Traveling somewhere should sometimes matter because distance, routes, discoveries, preparation, and returning home give places identity.

4. **No endless Kill 10 Wolves / Collect 12 Pelts / Click 8 Crystals quest design.** Repetition cannot substitute for gameplay. That's why our Hunting contracts became actual problems to investigate rather than counters to fill.

5. **No combat where the optimal strategy is just spam your strongest attack until the health bar disappears.** Combat needs decisions. This eventually became our hex-grid doctrine: positioning, movement, terrain, enemy behavior, weapon geometry, telegraphs, and timing should matter.

6. **No giant HP-sponge enemies pretending to be interesting bosses.** More health isn't a mechanic. A miniboss should change how you think or move. That's why Mossback's identity became the telegraphed charge and obstacle collision rather than “Normal Deer, But 8,000 HP.”

7. **No meaningless RNG overriding good tactical decisions.** We specifically leaned away from ordinary attacks randomly missing. If you correctly create an opening and choose a valid attack, an invisible dice roll shouldn't arbitrarily say “nope.” Randomness can exist where it produces interesting adaptation, but not casino fog over basic competence.

8. **No fake choices.** Listen / Leave is exactly the sort of thing we don't want. If there's no meaningful decision, just continue the conversation. Give choices when the player can actually express an attitude, ask something, accept/refuse, lie, joke, threaten, investigate, etc.

9. **No player agency that exists only until the designer doesn't like the player's answer.** If we say you can refuse Marlow, you can refuse Marlow. If you can leave the inn, the door works. If you can attack the Mooncalf, the game doesn't secretly disable your weapon. Choices can have consequences.

10. **No invisible walls masquerading as storytelling.** Whenever possible, restrictions should make sense in-world. You can't initially enter Sylvie's kitchen because you're a stranger walking into somebody's working kitchen, and Garrick reacts. Later you're invited inside. The relationship changes the boundary.

11. **No “chosen legendary hero” treatment before the player earns it.** Reputation, trust, titles, fear, respect, and access should be earned.

12. **No payoff without setup.** Deaths, villains, betrayals, emotional scenes, legendary equipment, major reveals, and victories need groundwork. The game shouldn't demand that the player care because dramatic music started.

13. **No world that exists frozen solely for the player.** We want the impression that people have jobs, relationships, and problems beyond standing under punctuation marks waiting for us. The mill actually runs again. Farmers recover. Animals can relocate. NPC relationships continue.

14. **No quest/story continuity amnesia.** The game should not forget completed quests, reset important progress, reintroduce dead characters, or otherwise make chronology meaningless. The world should remember important state.

15. **No labyrinth of inconsistent navigation.** Locations need coherent spatial relationships and understandable ways of reaching them. Avoid spaghetti-world navigation where different menus/NPCs point to conflicting versions of the same place.

16. **No inconsistent UI language.** The same function should generally look and behave the same way. Our weirdness belongs in the world, not in figuring out which X closes today's menu.

17. **No interface replacing the world.** Don't make Hunting “activate detective vision and follow orange footprints.” Don't make Cooking “select recipe → CRAFT.” Don't make exploration “click destination from list.” The physical world should carry as much information and interaction as practical.

18. **No pixel hunting either.** “World first” doesn't mean hiding a brown feather against a brown floor and laughing when the player can't find it. Important interactables can have subtle contextual assistance.

19. **No single mandatory clue that can brick an investigation.** Multiple pieces of evidence should support important deductions. Missing optional information can cost knowledge or an advantage, but shouldn't leave the player wandering for 45 minutes because they missed Feather #7.

20. **No tutorial avalanche.** Don't unlock twelve tabs, six currencies, crafting, pets, housing, professions, enchanting, and a battle pass in the first hour. Teach one mental model at a time, when the fiction gives the player a reason to care about it.

21. **No tutorial that tells you exactly where to click without teaching why.** A tutorial should teach understanding that transfers to future situations, not train the player to obey glowing arrows.

22. **No excessive hand-holding that makes discovery impossible.** Quest markers shouldn't reduce “find the apple tree” to walking directly toward an arrow every time. Give enough information for the player to reason and navigate.

23. **No systems that exist only because RPGs are “supposed” to have them.** If Smithing, Cooking, Hunting, Taming, housing, armor, crafting, etc. exist, each needs an actual role in the game and connections to other systems. We don't need feature Pokémon where we gotta catch every genre mechanic.

24. **No isolated skill bars whose entire purpose is making numbers increase.** Skills should change what you understand, notice, harvest, create, access, or accomplish. Higher Hunting, for example, can make an old creature newly interesting because you now recognize signs or harvest parts you couldn't use before.

25. **No meaningless grinding.** Repetition is fine when mastery, strategy, changing circumstances, useful output, or long-term goals make it engaging. Repeating the identical zero-decision action hundreds of times because a progress bar demands it is not.

26. **No misery zone.** Don't combine high attention requirement + trivial decision-making + weak payoff. If we're demanding the player's attention, give their brain something worthwhile to do.

27. **No arbitrary daily progression caps designed to stop the player from playing.** If somebody is having fun progressing for four hours, the game shouldn't suddenly say, “That's enough enjoyment for today.”

28. **No monetization manipulating the design.** No intentionally irritating inventory full of unusable premium gear, paid solutions to manufactured inconvenience, gambling boxes, FOMO machinery, premium refreshes, or systems made worse so money can fix them. Design decisions should answer “is this fun/good for the game?” first.

29. **No loot diarrhea.** We don't want enemies vomiting six color-coded pieces of disposable equipment. Equipment should be relatively meaningful, understandable, and preferably come from believable sources.

30. **No rarity color replacing thought.** Purple shouldn't automatically mean “throw away the interesting blue thing.” That's part of why we've avoided Common → Rare → Epic → Legendary as our opening equipment language.

31. **No runaway stat inflation.** We deliberately started with numbers such as 30 HP and 6 damage because we want +1 to matter. We don't want 138,729 damage becoming 146,251 damage and nobody feeling the difference.

32. **No recipe rarity automatically determining food quality.** A master chef should be capable of making extraordinary eggs. Owning the “Legendary Dragon Emperor Recipe” shouldn't magically make an incompetent cook superior to Sylvie.

33. **No crafting that is merely ingredients → click Craft → item appears when the activity is supposed to matter.** Potion Making, Cooking, Hunting, and eventually Smithing should involve the meaningful parts of those fantasies, while avoiding turning each into twelve unrelated minigames.

34. **No friction just for friction's sake, but also no crusade to eliminate every second of friction.** Travel, preparation, gathering, and quiet moments are valuable when they build place, anticipation, mastery, planning, or payoff. Delete meaningless friction, not texture.

35. **No anti-climactic endings to adventures.** If we build tension for a hunt, dungeon, boss, discovery, or quest, the game needs to acknowledge its conclusion. The watermill starting back up is a good example. The world itself provides the payoff.

36. **No joke saturation.** We absolutely want humor, weird creatures, dry dialogue, and ridiculous little discoveries. But if every NPC is trying to win Joke of the Minute, nothing has weight. Serious, mundane, and quiet moments create contrast so the strange stuff actually lands.

37. **No copying another game's identity at the expense of our own.** We can learn from AdventureQuest, RuneScape, Zelda, Souls games, D&D, and others, but every borrowed lesson has to become part of our RPG, not cosplay the source.

38. **No moving to the next shiny system before the current one works.** Build iteratively, yes. But don't leave a graveyard of half-working systems while sprinting toward Taming, Smithing, sailing, kingdoms, dragon breeding, or any other exciting future feature.

## Positive rule underneath almost everything

**Make the player care about what they're doing, understand why they're doing it, give them something meaningful to think about while doing it, and let the world acknowledge that they did it.**

## Later discoveries approved as project-wide doctrine

### Intrinsic-fun test

Progression and rewards should reinforce enjoyable activities, not compensate for boring ones.

When evaluating a core activity, ask:

> **If XP, loot, and progression numbers were temporarily hidden, would doing this once still be interesting?**

Hunting should be interesting because investigating and understanding the situation is engaging. Cooking should be interesting because reading and manipulating the food is engaging. Combat should be interesting because the battlefield creates meaningful decisions. Rewards should make players want to return, not serve as life support for an activity that is dull underneath.

### Systemic-discovery principle

Prefer understandable, consistent rules that players can combine and discover for themselves over bespoke scripted solutions.

If multiple systems logically interact, allow unexpected-but-valid solutions where practical. Do not immediately tutorialize every useful interaction the player could discover.

This does **not** require a universal physics sandbox. Start with a few strong rules, make them reliable, and let combinations emerge naturally.

### Quality over content volume

Do not measure the game's value by hours, map icons, quest counts, collectible counts, or system count.

A smaller amount of consistently strong content is preferable to a larger amount padded with repetitive filler, forced backtracking, artificial roadblocks, or systems whose main purpose is extending playtime.

The project may eventually become large, but size should be the consequence of accumulating worthwhile content, not a production target by itself.

### Discovery before instruction

Exploration and learning should leave room for genuine player discovery. Give players enough information to reason, then allow them to form their own questions and find answers through play.

Clear rules and readable threats are good. Automatically exposing the optimal solution is not.

This principle applies to exploration, Hunting, combat, crafting, quests, and world interaction.

### Expressive systems create durable replayability

Long-term variety should come as much as possible from reusable systems that interact in interesting ways, rather than only from an ever-growing pile of bespoke one-use content.

A small number of expressive rules can support many memorable situations when enemies, terrain, tools, player abilities, and world state combine differently.

This is not an excuse to build giant generalized frameworks before they are needed. Prove each system in the playable game first.

## Working test for future proposals

When reviewing a new system, quest, progression layer, or feature, ask:

1. What meaningful decision or experience does this create?
2. Would the activity still be worth doing once without a reward bar attached?
3. Does it respect the player's time?
4. Does it strengthen the world or replace the world with UI?
5. Does it preserve prior progress and knowledge where practical?
6. Does it create room for discovery or merely prescribe a solution?
7. Does it belong in this RPG, or are we adding it because another RPG had it?
8. Is the current game strong enough to justify building this next?
